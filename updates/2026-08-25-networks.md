We're still in the process of gathering together materials and figuring out how to approach this mountainous topic of ours. In this post, I (SG) want to explore the Himalayan Database.

The [Himalayan Database](https://himalayandatabase.com/), compiled at first by the journalist Elizabeth Hawley and now its own non-profit, is an astonishing source of data on the mountains and the history of climbing. I (SG) downloaded it and have been exploring, searching by peak. I retrieved some 2043 records concerning expeditions to Everest - things like the nationality of the expedition leader, the route(s) taken, times and dates of various camps, success and failures (and a codification of why an expedition failed), publications related to a particular expedition... there's a lot of material. In the interface for a given expedition, you can also read notes detailing things like equipment abandoned, or the behaviour of various members of the expedition (often a result of low-oxygen and frankly things that scare me completely: wandering off into the snow, undressed for the weather, stumbling in the dark searching for a tent...)

I am currently working on building some visualizations and analyzes to get a sense of what the intersection of different national cultures, different climbing cultures, different routes, over time, looks like in this data. Richard Salisbury, Elizabeth Hawley, and Billi Bierling have already crunched most of the numbers in ['The Himalaya by the Numbers: A Statistical Analysis of Mountaineering in the Nepal Himalaya, 1950-2024'](https://himalayandatabase.com/downloads/HimalayaByNbrs%20(1950-2024).pdf), which is a free e-book. I'm not trying to replicate that, but more get my own sense of the mountain and its material culture, which is a bit of a different concern and not one that is obviously dealt with in the database. I'll do a follow-up post here on what I see when I count from that perspective, but in this post I want to think about networks. (If you're new to networks, [Ahnert et al.](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108866804) is a good place to start.)

Looking at the data, it occurred to me that many climbers participate in many different expeditions; that expedition culture would be a function of that composition; that climbers learn how-to-be through other climbers; and that there would be associations of different routes/camps over time with these networks.

The data that I downloaded has a column for the expedition ID, and a column with a climber ID. _Expeditions - to - climber_ has the form of a classic bimodal network, and sometimes visualizing that alone can give you interesting insight. Some caveats though: most social network metrics are geared towards networks where there is one kind of node, and one kind of relationship. Second: a *lot* of people have climbed everest: in the data I downloaded, there are 12635 individuals who have climbed Everest over 2400 expeditions. That bimodal network looks like a giant hairball. The solution is to create _two_ networks: one where climbers are connected to other climbers by virtue of participating in the _same_ expedition, and one where expeditions are connected by virtue of common climbers. We turn one kind of node into the _edge_ that connects the _other_ kind of node.

>Aside: the thought occurs that one could view these networks as a way of surfacing the _infrastructure_ of climbing, in the sense that Susan Leigh Star wrote in 'The Ethnography of Infrastructure' (1999). What you see viz. infrastrucutre depends on how you look; when infrastructure works you can't see it, don't notice it. But when it breaks... [Liu et al, writing in Critical Infrastructure Studies (2026)](https://dhdebates.gc.cuny.edu/read/critical-infrastructure-studies-and-digital-humanities/section/5981ad1c-2039-45d0-968f-b232ae58f7e0) write, 'infrastructure toggles between invisibility and visibility depending on how one person is positioned relative to it versus another'. When you've got relative positionings... that's a network. They go on: '...infrastructure studies fuses the concepts of brokenness and social relationality to depict infrastructure as systematically antisystemic. Infrastructure is a system that works for some, but for that very reason, is broken for others. More cruelly, as in the case of city benches made with protrusions to prevent unhoused poor people from sleeping on them, it is designed to be broken for others.' So: for whom does the infrastructure of climbing at Everest work? For whom is it broken? How could we spot that, at a distance, from this data we have to hand?

We'll leave that thought aside for now, and take a look to see what we may see.

## Expeditions to Expeditions

Here is what we see when we look at how Expeditions connect, coloured by the nationality of the expedition (where nationality is what is encoded in the databae):

![](images/exp-to-exp1.png)

![](images/nations.png) 

Clearly, the US has sent the majority of expeditions, and expeditions-by-nation are easy to separate out: while climbers might participate in expeditions led by other countries, they mostly just shift about from one national expedition to another. 

The size of the nodes is a measurement called 'betweenness', meaning, those nodes sit on the majority of shortest paths between any two nodes in the network. Think of it like this: it's high school again, and you're a member of the musical theatre group. But you're also on the soccer team. You're not popular in either group, but you're the only member of both. So you hear all of the latest gossip from the jocks and you can control what the musical theatre kids hear; you hear all of the latest neat music and pass on interesting bits to the jocks. You are in-between both worlds, and in a position to control interactions between both groups.

Looking at the expeditions this way, the most 'between' expedition was EVER88101, the China-Japan-Nepal Friendship Expedition to Qomolangma/Sagarmatha in 1988 [article here](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/12198927103). What makes it the most between? Partly a function of the size of the climbing team. There were 99 members of the expedition (at least), and then several hundred support staff and military. The expedition attempted multiple routes. With those climbers dispersing to other expeditions afterwards (or having climbed everest before), this particular expedition encapsulates perhaps a nodal point for the dispersal of climbing knowledge. It's not for nothing that this expedition (and the second most between expedition, EVER90103) happen at the beginning of the period of commercialization of Everest climbing. But, at least on this first pass, there does not seem to be any obvious associations between year or nationality and betweeness centrality.

Betweeness might in fact be the wrong metric. Another metric we can look at is 'eigenvector centrality'. In essence, this is a metric that says a node's importance is a function of being connected to other important nodes. When we redo the visualization to scale node size according to this metric, we see something interesting: a whole host of expeditions led by New Zealanders (highest score: EVER07153, from 2007. Indeed, the top ten all are New Zealand, and all from 1997-2014). Is this implying that New Zealanders' knowledge is what ties expeditions together?

## People to People 

When we invert the network so that climbers are tied to other climbers by virtue of being on a shared expedition (expeditions are now the 'edges' or ties), here is what we see.

![](images/people-to-people.png)

The colouring is by citizenship. There is a network statistic called 'modularity' which examines the structure of the network to identify sub-patterns of nodes that are connected to each other more similarly than they are to the rest of the network. You could call this 'community detection'. When we do that on this graph, there is almost a total overlap with the citizenship of the climber, ie, climbers from Nepal are more connected to each other in ways that are different from how they connect to the rest of the network.

> Another aside: after calculating modularity, I did a simple chi-squared test to see if there is an association between 'modularity' (a measure of network structure) and 'citizen' and if my math is correct, there is definitely a strong signal. Digging a little deeper, within the various 'modules' or detected communities, the strongest positive signals were France, Ecuador, Aremenia, and Turkey (turning up in a module more often than expected), while the strongest negative signals were USA and India (turning up in a module less often than expected). I take this to mean that individuals in the first group are more likely to be climbing with co-nationals, while individuals in the second group are less likely to be climbing in single-nation groups. But... take with a grain of salt. This is all just a first pass on the data and I'm writing my thoughts out loud as I go...  

When we look at the individual climbers who are most between, we find Russell Reginal Brice (New Zealand), Loveraj Singh Dharmshaktu (India), Josette Valloton (Switzerland), Tsering Samdrup (Tsering Sangzhu) (China), Mark Scott Tucker (USA) as the five most 'between' climbers. Interesting, but doesn't move us forward. 

![](images/eigen-people.png)

When we consider eigenvector centrality, we do spot something interesting: the top 15 are all Sherpa (with citizenship listed in the database as China or India or Nepal). And you don't get until position 165 to see a Westerner in the list.

## So What Does This All Mean?

What does this mean, if we're interested in the material culture of being on Everest? Certainly, we're going to see very distinct national trends in that material culture, but I suspect they'll separate out in subtle ways, e.g. American kit versus British kit for instance. But I find my thoughts circling around eigenvector centrality - and other possible measures - that point to the hidden infrastructures of climbing: kit _and_ people. Logistics chains. The history of climbing (at least, as written in Anglophone scholarship), and the accounts of climbing written by climbers themselves, often give short shift to those folks who make it all possible. (We note a [recent article about a health study conducted at high altitude - base camp and above - where the scientists put climbers on stationary bikes to measure oxygenation and so on](https://www.zmescience.com/feature-post/pieces/xtreme-everest-limits-human-body/): who carried the damned bikes over the Khumbu icefall?). It's not novel to point that out. But it is interesting to see that something like network analysis seems to pick it out, from simple membership lists. What else might we see? Stay tuned.
